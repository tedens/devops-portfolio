What this tool assumes

Worth saying plainly, because most size guides don't and that's why people turn up with a van that won't empty.

It packs imperfectly, on purpose

The layout comes from a rectangle-packing algorithm, not from dividing your volume by the floor area. Real units have gaps, because real furniture doesn't tessellate. A guide that assumes a perfect fit will always tell you a size too small.

Against the published size guides most operators use, this lands on the same answer for all eight of the typical loads above.

Eight-foot ceilings, and we use them

Indoor units are 8 ft to the ceiling; drive-up units are 9 ft. The planner assumes 8, and it assumes you stack: boxes go on top of anything with a rigid flat top, which is the single biggest difference between a well-packed unit and a badly packed one. Untick “stack where it's sensible” and watch the recommendation jump one or two sizes. That gap is real.

The door is a separate problem from the floor

Units 5 ft wide have a 3 ft swing door. Units 10 ft wide have an 8 ft roller. If the narrowest dimension of something is wider than the opening, it does not matter how much floor is spare . It isn't going in. The planner checks this before it checks anything else, and says so.

Cars need room to get out of

A compact car is about 15 ft by 6. A 10 × 15 is 15 ft deep, so on paper it fits, and then you can't open the door. Vehicles are packed with 18 in of clearance all round, drawn as the dashed outline, which is why the planner sends you to a 10 × 20. Vehicles also need a drive-up unit.

It's a guide, not a contract

If you're between two sizes, take the bigger one for the first month and move down once you've seen it. Moving between units on site is free and takes an afternoon. Nobody has ever regretted the larger unit on moving day.
